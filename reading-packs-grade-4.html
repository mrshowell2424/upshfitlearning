1. The Ship in the Desert

Higher version · Lexile 880 · 312 words

A In the town of Moynaq, Uzbekistan, rusting fishing boats sit in sand. There is no water for sixty miles. Fifty years ago, Moynaq was a busy port on the Aral Sea, the fourth largest lake in the world.

B The Aral Sea was fed by two rivers. In the 1960s, planners decided to redirect those rivers into canals to irrigate cotton fields in the desert. The cotton grew. The sea began to shrink.

C At first the change was slow enough to ignore. Then it accelerated. By 2000 the sea had split in two. By 2014 the eastern half had dried completely for the first time in six hundred years. The lake had lost about ninety percent of its water.

D The damage went far beyond the shoreline. The exposed seabed was full of salt and pesticide residue from the cotton fields. Wind lifted that dust and dropped it on towns hundreds of miles away. Rates of lung disease and certain cancers climbed. Twenty-four species of fish that lived nowhere else disappeared, and sixty thousand fishing jobs went with them.

E The weather changed too. A large lake moderates temperature. Without it, summers in the region grew hotter and winters grew colder.

F Not all of it is lost. In 2005 Kazakhstan finished a dam across the northern section. Water levels there rose, the salt level dropped, and fish returned. Boats are working the northern Aral again. The southern half is still desert.

2. The Woman Who Mapped the Ocean Floor

Higher version · Lexile 900 · 316 words

A In 1952, almost nobody knew what the bottom of the Atlantic Ocean looked like. Ships had taken depth readings along their routes for decades, but the readings sat in notebooks as columns of numbers.

B Marie Tharp was a geologist at Columbia University. She was not allowed on the research ships; at the time, women were considered bad luck at sea. So she stayed on shore and turned the numbers into pictures. Line by line, she drew the profile of the sea floor.

C What appeared was not a flat plain. It was a mountain range running down the middle of the Atlantic, longer than the Andes, Rockies, and Himalayas combined. And down the center of that range ran a deep valley.

D The valley was the problem. A crack that long suggested the sea floor was splitting apart, which supported the idea that continents move. Most geologists rejected that idea outright. Tharp’s colleague Bruce Heezen called her finding "girl talk" and told her to do it over.

E She did it over. The valley was still there. Then an assistant plotted the locations of Atlantic earthquakes on the same map, and the earthquakes fell in a line directly inside Tharp’s valley. Heezen changed his mind.

F Their maps, published over the next twenty years, helped convince a generation of scientists that the plates of the Earth move. Tharp’s name did not appear on the first paper announcing the discovery.

3. Why Venice Is Sinking

Higher version · Lexile 890 · 318 words

A Venice, Italy is built on 118 small islands in a lagoon. Its buildings do not rest on rock. They rest on millions of wooden posts driven down into mud, a technique that has held for more than a thousand years.

B The city is sinking and the water is rising, and those two problems have completely different causes.

C The sinking has two sources. Some of it is natural: the soft ground compacts slowly under the weight of the city. The rest was self-inflicted. In the middle of the twentieth century, factories on the mainland pumped groundwater out from under the lagoon, and the ground above the emptied space settled. Venice dropped about twelve centimeters before the pumping was banned in the 1970s.

D The rising is global. Warmer oceans expand, and melting ice adds volume. Sea level in the northern Adriatic has climbed roughly thirty centimeters over the last century.

E Combined, the two effects flood St. Mark’s Square more than sixty times a year. A century ago it flooded fewer than ten.

F Venice’s answer is a set of 78 steel gates that lie flat on the sea floor at the three entrances to the lagoon. When a high tide is forecast, compressed air pushes the water out of the gates and they swing up into a wall. The system first protected the city in 2020. It cost more than five billion euros and took eighteen years to finish.

4. The Night the Radio Lied

Higher version · Lexile 910 · 322 words

A On October 30, 1938, the Mercury Theatre broadcast a radio play based on H.G. Wells’s novel The War of the Worlds. The script was written as a series of news bulletins interrupting a program of dance music.

B The format mattered. Listeners heard what sounded like a real reporter describing a cylinder opening in a New Jersey field, then a heat ray, then the broadcast itself collapsing into static. An announcer stated at the opening that the program was fiction, but many people tuned in late.

C Newspapers the next morning described mass panic: crowds in the streets, jammed switchboards, families fleeing cities. The story became famous, and it is still repeated in classrooms today.

D Later researchers questioned it. Ratings data showed that relatively few people were listening, because a popular comedy program held most of the audience in that hour. Surveys taken afterward found that most listeners who did hear the show understood it was a play.

E So why did the panic story spread? Newspapers at the time were losing advertising money to radio. A story about radio being reckless and dangerous served their interests. Historians now believe the papers inflated a small number of frightened phone calls into a national event.

F The broadcast is still a useful lesson, though perhaps not the one usually taught. The audience was not as gullible as the story claims. The people who repeated the story were.

5. Reading the Rings

Higher version · Lexile 870 · 324 words

A Cut across a tree trunk and you see rings. Each ring is one year of growth: a pale band of fast spring growth and a dark band of slow summer growth. Counting them gives the tree’s age. Measuring them gives much more.

B A wide ring means a good year with plenty of water. A narrow ring means drought. Because every tree in a region lives through the same weather, trees of the same species in the same place share the same pattern of wide and narrow rings, a little like a barcode.

C That shared pattern is what makes the method powerful. A scientist can overlap the rings of a living tree with the outer rings of an older dead log, then overlap that log with an even older beam, and keep stepping backward. In the American Southwest, this chain of overlaps reaches back more than two thousand years.

D Archaeologists use it to date buildings. A wooden beam in a cliff dwelling can be matched to the master pattern, which gives the exact year that tree was cut. That is how we know the largest structures at Chaco Canyon went up in bursts of building rather than steadily.

E Climate scientists use it differently. The rings preserve a record of rainfall from long before anyone was measuring rainfall. Tree rings show that a drought in the Southwest in the late 1200s lasted more than twenty years, which matches the period when many cliff dwellings were abandoned.

F The tree does not know it is keeping records. It simply grows more in a wet year than a dry one. Everything else is us learning to read.

6. The Clock That Found the Longitude

Higher version · Lexile 890 · 316 words

A For centuries, a ship at sea could tell how far north or south it was by measuring the sun. Nobody could reliably tell how far east or west. Ships missed islands by hundreds of miles. Crews starved. In 1707, four British warships ran into rocks off England and about fourteen hundred men died.

B The math was not the hard part. The Earth turns fifteen degrees every hour. If a sailor knew the exact time at home and the exact time where the ship was, the difference would give the longitude. The problem was knowing the time back home.

C Clocks of the day used a swinging pendulum. On a rolling ship a pendulum is useless. Heat, cold, and salt air threw the mechanism off further. Britain offered a prize worth millions in today’s money to anyone who could solve it.

D Most experts worked on maps of the moon and stars. A carpenter named John Harrison built a clock instead. He replaced the pendulum with springs that balanced each other, so rocking did not matter. He used two metals that expanded at different rates, so the clock corrected itself as the temperature changed.

E His fourth version was the size of a large pocket watch. On a voyage to Jamaica in 1761 it lost about five seconds in eighty-one days.

F The board that judged the prize was full of astronomers, and they kept demanding new trials. Harrison spent forty years on the clocks and most of his life arguing for the money. He was finally paid at the age of eighty.

Answer: Because the two metals expanded at different rates, they pulled against each other as the temperature changed, which corrected the mechanism and kept the clock running at the same speed in heat and cold.

7. The River That Wants to Move

Higher version · Lexile 900 · 318 words

A Rivers do not stay where we put them. Over thousands of years the Mississippi has swung back and forth across southern Louisiana, building land in one place and abandoning it for another. Roughly every thousand years it finds a shorter path to the sea and takes it.

B By the 1950s it had found one. A smaller river called the Atchafalaya offered a route to the Gulf less than half as long as the Mississippi’s own. Water flows toward the steeper, shorter path. Each year a little more of the Mississippi went that way.

C The consequences would have been enormous. Baton Rouge and New Orleans sit on the Mississippi. So do oil refineries, chemical plants, and the port that handles much of the country’s grain. If the river left, their water would turn salty and their shipping channel would become a shallow bay.

D So the Army Corps of Engineers built the Old River Control Structure: a set of concrete gates that lets exactly thirty percent of the flow into the Atchafalaya and holds the rest in the Mississippi. It opened in 1963.

E In 1973 a flood nearly ended it. Water scoured a hole under one of the structures deep enough to swallow a building. Engineers dropped boulders in for weeks. The structure held, but a wing of it collapsed, and afterward the Corps built a second structure beside the first as backup.

F The river has not stopped trying. Every year the gates hold a continent’s worth of water on a path it no longer wants to take.

8. The Vault Inside the Mountain

Higher version · Lexile 870 · 312 words

A On a frozen island between Norway and the North Pole, a concrete wedge sticks out of a mountainside. A tunnel runs four hundred feet into the rock. At the end are three rooms holding seeds.

B The Svalbard Global Seed Vault is a backup copy of the world’s crops. Countries send duplicate samples of their seeds, sealed in foil packets and boxed. It holds more than a million varieties: thousands of kinds of rice, wheat, beans, and potatoes that most people have never heard of.

C Variety is the point. A crop grown from one single variety can be wiped out by one disease. When a blight destroyed Ireland’s potatoes in the 1840s, nearly all of them were the same kind. Old and rare varieties carry traits, like resistance to a fungus or tolerance for salty soil, that plant breeders may need later.

D The location was chosen for physics, not scenery. The rock stays below freezing on its own, so even if the power failed the seeds would stay cold for weeks. The vault sits well above sea level.

E It has been used once. The seed bank in Aleppo, Syria was damaged in the civil war, and researchers withdrew samples from Svalbard to restart their collection in Lebanon and Morocco. They later sent replacements back.

F No country owns the seeds inside. Each depositor keeps ownership of its own boxes, and only that depositor can open them. The vault is a safe-deposit box, not a library.

9. The Girl Who Named a Planet

Higher version · Lexile 860 · 306 words

A In March 1930, an eleven-year-old named Venetia Burney was eating breakfast in Oxford, England. Her grandfather read aloud from the newspaper: astronomers in Arizona had found a ninth planet, and it did not have a name yet.

B Venetia had been studying Greek and Roman myths at school. The planet was the farthest from the sun, cold and dark and never visited. She suggested Pluto, the Roman god of the underworld.

C Her grandfather had worked at the Bodleian Library and knew an astronomy professor. He passed the suggestion along. The professor cabled it to the observatory in Arizona.

D The staff there were already voting on names. Pluto won unanimously, partly for the myth and partly because the first two letters were the initials of Percival Lowell, the astronomer whose observatory had begun the search. The name was announced on May 1, 1930.
