1. The Race to Nome

Higher version · Lexile 760 · 281 words

A In January of 1925, a doctor in Nome, Alaska sent an urgent telegram. Children in his town were sick with diphtheria. Without medicine, the disease could kill most of them. The nearest supply was a thousand miles away.

B Nome was frozen in. The port was iced over until spring. The two airplanes in the territory had open cockpits and engines that would not start in the cold. That left one way in: dog sled.

C The plan was a relay. The medicine traveled by train as far as the rails went, to a town called Nenana. From there, twenty mushers and about 150 sled dogs would carry it in stages across 674 miles of frozen Alaska.

D The weather was brutal. Temperatures dropped near sixty degrees below zero. One musher, Leonhard Seppala, took a shortcut straight across the frozen sea ice of Norton Sound during a blizzard. If the ice had broken, his whole team would have gone into the water.

E The final stretch belonged to a musher named Gunnar Kaasen and his lead dog, Balto. Wind flipped the sled and buried the package of medicine in the snow. Kaasen dug it out with his bare hands. He reached Nome on the morning of February 2nd.

F The relay took five and a half days. The medicine arrived frozen but unbroken. A statue of Balto still stands in Central Park in New York, and every year the Iditarod sled dog race follows part of the same trail.

Answer: Because temperatures were near sixty below and a blizzard was blowing, the mushers risked frostbite, and Seppala risked breaking through the sea ice with his entire team.

2. Why Your Stomach Growls

Higher version · Lexile 780 · 268 words

A You are sitting in a quiet classroom and your stomach makes a long, loud noise. Everyone hears it. The sound has less to do with hunger than you might think.

B Your stomach and intestines are muscular tubes. About every ninety minutes, whether you have eaten or not, those muscles squeeze in a wave that pushes everything forward. The wave is called peristalsis. It works the way you squeeze toothpaste out of a tube.

C Inside those tubes there is always some liquid and some gas. When the muscles squeeze, the gas and liquid get pushed through narrow spaces. That is what makes the sound. Your body is not empty and rumbling. It is full and moving.

D So why does it seem louder when you are hungry? There are two reasons. Food muffles the noise, the way a full water bottle sloshes less than an empty one. And when you have not eaten for a couple of hours, your body runs an extra strong wave to sweep out leftovers.

E A growling stomach is not a problem to fix. It is a sign that the machinery is running.

4 (vocabulary): peristalsis: Peristalsis is the wave of squeezing that pushes food through your stomach and intestines.
muffles: Muffles means makes a sound quieter. Food in the stomach makes the noise quieter.

Answer: Because there is no food to muffle the sound and the body runs an extra strong cleaning wave, an empty stomach growls louder than a full one.

3. The Hats That Almost Ended the Birds

Higher version · Lexile 790 · 262 words

A In the 1890s, the most fashionable hats in America were covered in feathers. Not small feathers. Whole wings. Sometimes an entire stuffed bird sat on the brim.

B The demand was enormous. Hunters shot herons, egrets, and terns by the millions. Egrets were hunted hardest, because their finest feathers grow only during nesting season. Killing the adults left the chicks to starve. Some species came close to disappearing.

C Two women in Boston, Harriet Hemenway and Minna Hall, decided to act. Neither could vote. So they used what they did have: an invitation list. They asked the wealthiest women in the city to tea and asked them to stop wearing feathers.

D It worked. Hundreds agreed. The group they formed became the Massachusetts Audubon Society. Similar groups appeared in other states, and together they pushed for laws.

E In 1918, Congress passed a law making it illegal to hunt most wild birds for their feathers. Egret populations recovered. The next time you see a snowy egret standing in shallow water, remember that it is there partly because of a tea party.

4. Cities Under Our Feet

Higher version · Lexile 770 · 266 words

A Step on a sidewalk crack in summer and there is a good chance you are standing over a city. Ant colonies can run for yards underground, with rooms, roads, and jobs.

B A colony begins with a single queen. After her mating flight she lands, breaks off her own wings, digs a small chamber, and lays her first eggs. She feeds those first larvae using her wing muscles, which she will never need again.

C Once workers hatch, they take over. Some dig. Some forage for food. Some care for the young. Some do almost nothing for days at a time, which scientists think is a reserve crew for emergencies.

D The rooms have purposes. There are nurseries held at a steady temperature, food storage rooms, and a waste room kept far from everything else. Leafcutter ants add a farm room where they grow a fungus on chewed leaves. The fungus, not the leaves, is their food.

E No ant is in charge. The queen lays eggs; she does not give orders. Each ant follows simple rules and responds to chemical signals from its neighbors. Out of thousands of small decisions, a city runs.

5. The Glue That Failed

Higher version · Lexile 750 · 279 words

A In 1968, a scientist named Spencer Silver was trying to invent a very strong glue. He failed. What he made instead was a glue that stuck lightly and peeled off without leaving a mark.

B Silver thought it was useful. Nobody else did. For five years he gave talks inside his own company trying to find someone who wanted a weak glue. No one took it.

C Then a coworker named Art Fry got annoyed at church. He sang in the choir, and the paper scraps he used to mark the hymns kept sliding out of his book. He remembered Silver’s glue.

D Fry coated small pieces of paper with it. The markers stayed in the book, then peeled out clean. He made batches for people around the office, and those people would not stop asking for more.

E The first stores barely sold any. So in 1980 the company mailed free samples to offices in one city. Nine out of ten people who tried them ordered more. The notes went on sale everywhere that year.

F A glue that was too weak became one of the most common objects in the world. Silver had not failed. He had solved a problem nobody had asked about yet.

6. The Town That Moved

7. The Dogs That Find Whales

Higher version · Lexile 780 · 276 words

A Scientists who study killer whales have a problem. The whales live underwater. You cannot weigh one, check its health, or find out what it ate. Most of what a researcher wants to know is out of reach.

B There is one exception. Whale scat, which is whale waste, floats for about half an hour before it sinks. A sample tells researchers what the whale ate, whether it is pregnant, and how stressed it is.

C Finding it is the hard part. A boat crew scanning the water might spot one sample in a long day. So researchers turned to dogs.

D A dog named Tucker was trained to smell whale scat from up to a mile away. He stood at the bow of the boat and leaned toward the scent. The crew steered wherever he leaned. When the boat got close, his body language changed, and the crew scooped the sample out of the water.

E Dogs like Tucker found four times more samples than people scanning alone. What the samples showed mattered: these whales were not starving from a shortage of salmon, the way scientists had assumed. They were eating, but they were stressed by boat traffic.

F The dogs never see a whale. They only smell what one left behind.

8. How a Seed Crosses an Ocean

Higher version · Lexile 760 · 249 words

A A new island rises out of the sea with no life on it at all. Within a few years, plants are growing there. They had to get there somehow, and plants cannot move.

B Wind carries the smallest seeds. Orchid seeds are as fine as dust. A single pod releases millions of them, and a few ride the wind for hundreds of miles.

C Birds carry others. A seed with a hook catches in feathers. A seed inside a berry gets eaten, rides in the bird’s gut for hours, and lands in a new place already surrounded by fertilizer.

D The ocean carries the biggest travelers. A coconut has a thick husk full of air pockets, so it floats, sometimes for months. Salt water cannot get through the shell to the seed inside. Coconuts have crossed thousands of miles of open water and sprouted on beaches where no coconut palm had ever grown.

E Not every traveler survives. Most seeds land in salt water or on bare rock and die. But a plant does not need most of its seeds to make it. It only needs one.

10. Why Bridges Have Gaps

Higher version · Lexile 770 · 267 words

A Walk across a long bridge and at some point you will step over a metal comb set into the road. The teeth fit together but do not touch. That gap is not damage. It is one of the most important parts of the bridge.

B Steel and concrete expand when they get hot and shrink when they get cold. The change is tiny for a short piece, but a bridge is not short. A steel span a mile long can grow more than a foot between a January night and a July afternoon.

C If both ends were bolted down, that extra length would have nowhere to go. The bridge would push against its own supports with enormous force. Concrete would crack. Steel would buckle.

D So engineers build in expansion joints. One end of the span rests on rollers or a sliding plate. The comb-shaped gap opens in winter and closes in summer, and the road stays smooth enough to drive over either way.

E The same idea shows up in sidewalks, railroad tracks, and pipelines. Anything long and rigid that sits outside needs somewhere to put the extra length. Engineers do not fight the expansion. They plan a place for it to go.
